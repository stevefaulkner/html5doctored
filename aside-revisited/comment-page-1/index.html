Aside Revisited

This is an update from the previous article since the spec has changed to allow aside to be used for secondary content, changing it's context depending on whether or not it is contained within article.

As the HTML5 specification is not yet final, changes should be expected in order to improve on the good bits and cut out the bad bits. aside, a misunderstood good bit, is such an element that has had another look and tweaked based on feedback from the web development community. In this article we take a look at what has changed.

Doctor! Doctor! I feel like I’ve read about this before!

Here at HTML5Doctor we have indeed already discussed aside in “Understanding Aside“, and if you have met me in person you’ll have likely been hit by my ranting about its definition. Its definition suggested that aside should be used for content that was tangentially related to the content surrounding it, such as related reading links and glossaries.

It did not appear to be an appropriate element for holding secondary content, typically known as “sidebars” which contain content related to the site, rather than related to the specific article. From the comments here and elsewhere, you can see this definition was not accepted by the community, and opened the debate of “what should we use for secondary content?”.

Aside Redefined

Aside is now acceptable to use for secondary content, such as a blogroll, groups of additional navigation and even advertising if that content is related to the page. When used within an article element, the contents should be specifically related to that article, for example a glossary. When using aside, please be aware of these changes of context.

The two uses of aside can be best illustrated with an example.

<body>
  <header>
    <h1>My Blog</h1>
  </header>
  <article>
    <h1>My Blog Post</h1>
    <p>Lorem ipsum dolor sit amet, consectetur adipisicing elit, sed do
    eiusmod tempor incididunt ut labore et dolore magna aliqua.</p>
    <aside>
      <!-- Since this aside is contained within an article, a parser
      should understand that the content of this aside is directly related
      to the article itself. -->
      <h1>Glossary</h1>
      <dl>
        <dt>Lorem</dt>
        <dd>ipsum dolor sit amet</dd>
      </dl>
    </aside>
  </article>
  <aside>
    <!-- This aside is outside of the article and is therefore content
    related to the page but not as tightly to the above article -->
    <h2>Blogroll</h2>
    <ul>
      <li><a href="#">My Friend</a></li>
      <li><a href="#">My Other Friend</a></li>
      <li><a href="#">My Best Friend</a></li>
    </ul>
  </aside>
</body>

Conclusion

This means that aside can now be used for secondary content. Please keep in mind that aside, and similarly secondary content, does not necessarily mean “sidebar”—the style of the content should not dictate the use of this element. If you have content which does not take as strong a role as your main content, but is related to the page or article, then aside is what you need, regardless of its visual design.

@Sidebar: “Sidebar” relates to the presentation of an element, rather than it’s content. Separation of content and presentation is vital, but to clarify: what you refer to as a “sidebar” is likely to be “secondary content”, and therefore <aside> is appropriate. Of course, if your “sidebar” is purely navigation that is key to the page, something like <nav> would be more appropriate. Presentation is irrelevant at the mark-up stage, so please refer to the content itself (”Primary Content”, “Secondary Content”, “Navigation”, etc) rather than how it could be styled.

@Kyle, Rachel, Gerrit & Sidebar: <aside> was originally called “sidebar” but was changed due to the presentational connotations with that name. How <aside> changes context depending on it’s parent elements is perfectly logical and is not the only new element that does so. For example, <header> and <footer> can exist directly in the <body>, in an <article>, or even in a <section>. The <header> of an <article> adopts a context that is dependant on that <article>, rather than the HTML document as a whole. Just like we don’t have a “subnav” element to differ from <nav>, adding an additional “sidebar” elment would be redundant.

@kimblim: Doh! Thanks for that, I’ve corrected the example

The element can be used for typographical effects like pull quotes or sidebars, for advertising, for groups of nav elements, and for other content that is considered separate from the main content of the page.

@Gerrit: It is acceptable to use multiple <aside> elements, particularly if you are grouping it’s contents by a particular theme. For example, an “Elsewhere” group of content that acts as a lifestream, plus a group of content directly related to the site (”Recent Posts”, “Tag Cloud”, etc).
